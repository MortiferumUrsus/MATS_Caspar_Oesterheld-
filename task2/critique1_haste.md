# Critique of Argument 1: "The haste consideration"

**The single issue.** The thought experiment does not compare *earlier time* with *later time*. It compares two
*activities*: two years spent recruiting a lifetime-equivalent altruist, versus a lifetime of doing as much good as you can,
whatever that turns out to be. The stipulation "the person you convince is at least as effective as you and would otherwise
have done nothing" does all the work, and it says nothing about the calendar. So the "lesson" — "you can do more good with
time in the present than with time in the future" — is not delivered by the comparison; it is a change of subject, and
everything downstream (the two years being "more valuable than any 2-year period in the future", the advice to weight the next
few years of a career especially heavily) inherits the gap. A definitional truth about which of two stipulated worlds is better
cannot carry a substantive claim about time. And since the stipulation is only "at least as much", even read as a claim about
time it yields no more than V(now) ≥ V(later), which time-neutrality satisfies.

**The time-shift test.** Take the argument's construction and move the recruiting window. World (1′): you do nothing altruistic
in years 20–22 of your career and otherwise do as much good as you can. World (2′): in years 20–22 you convince one person, at
least as effective as you, who would otherwise have done nothing, and you do nothing after year 22; by stipulation they do at
least as much good as you would have done after year 22 in (1′). Exactly as before, (2′) ≥ (1′) "by definition", and the
argument's own inference then yields: *years 20–22 are worth at least as much as all of the rest of your life after them*. The
same construction works for years 30–32. This does rank each window above what follows it, but only because the window is
*credited with* the entire remainder of the career: the recruit is stipulated to reproduce it, so of course the window outranks any
part of it. That is containment, not haste. The haste consideration says a *unit* of resource is worth more now than the same unit
later; the schema never compares same-use windows at all, since it only lower-bounds a recruiting window against direct work
afterwards. Nothing in the premises is indexed to "now".

**What the premises actually support, and the author's best reply.** Under the stipulation, recruiting at time t is worth what
you would have done from t + 2 to the end of your career. So recruiting at year 0 beats recruiting at year 20 by exactly the value
of what you would have done in years 2–22: a count of years covered, not a premium on early years. With constant annual output v
and horizon T this is (T − t − 2)·v, and two things follow. (a) Recruiting beats two years of direct work (2v) whenever more than
four years remain — a claim about the *leverage of an activity*. (b) Recruiting at year 0 yields (T − 2)·v against (T − 22)·v at
year 20. This is the author's strongest reply, and it should be conceded: sooner is better, for the same activity, on a
like-for-like basis. But it is a linear horizon effect — roughly a factor of two over twenty years of a forty-year career — not
"vastly", and it does not make the next two years worth more than the rest of a life spent the same way. It is smallest exactly
where the career advice bites: postponing recruitment from year 0 to year 4 for a degree costs 4/38 ≈ 10% under the same model.
And it is not what the passage argues from: the passage argues from the (2) ≥ (1) comparison, which is time-neutral.

**The practical implication collapses with it.** "If a career requires you to spend the next few years studying and doing nothing
altruistic, this is a major cost" is true only if those years would otherwise be spent on the high-leverage activity. By the
argument's own logic that is equally true of *any* years: the opportunity cost of study in career year 0 and in career year 20 is
the same lifetime-equivalent recruit, minus the linear horizon effect. The argument gives no reason to weight the next few years
specially; at most it says "recruit", and the only reason it can give for "soon" is the linear effect it never invokes.

**The natural repair does not rescue the passage.** One might reply that the real haste consideration is about compounding:
recruits recruit, so early resources grow exponentially and earlier is genuinely better. But the passage does not argue from
compounding: the recruit is valued at "at least as much as you would have done", whatever that is — a stipulation that already
absorbs any compounding you would have done — and the inequality is defended as holding "by definition", so it holds whether or not
anyone compounds. If compounding is the argument, then (i) the thought experiment is idle as evidence for haste, and (ii) the
argument needs an empirical premise the passage never states or defends: a high, sustained growth rate for the movement, with
saturation still far off. And if saturation is what makes the present special, the case for haste is an empirical claim about
where we sit on the growth curve, which the thought experiment does not touch. Either the stipulated comparison carries the
conclusion, and then it carries the wrong conclusion (leverage, not haste); or a growth premise carries it, and then the passage
has not argued for it at all.

**Conclusion.** Granting every stipulation, the argument shows that recruiting a lifetime-equivalent altruist beats direct work
whenever such a recruit is available. That is a claim about the leverage of an activity, not the value of a time period; the
"lesson" about time, and the career advice built on it, have no support in the text beyond a linear horizon effect the passage
never invokes.
