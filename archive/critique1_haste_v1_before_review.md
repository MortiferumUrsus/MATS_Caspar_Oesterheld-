# Critique of Argument 1: "The haste consideration"

**The single issue.** The thought experiment does not compare *earlier time* with *later time*. It compares two
*activities*: two years of recruiting a lifetime-equivalent altruist versus a lifetime of direct work. The stipulation
"the person you convince is at least as effective as you and would otherwise have done nothing" does all the work, and it is
completely time-neutral. So the "lesson" that "you can do more good with time in the present than with time in the future" does
not follow from the comparison; it is a change of subject. Everything downstream (the two years being "more valuable than any
2-year period in the future", the advice to weight the next few years of a career especially heavily) inherits the gap.

**Why the gap is real: the time-shift test.** Take the argument's construction and move the recruiting window. World (1′): you do
nothing altruistic in years 20–22 of your career and otherwise do as much good as you can. World (2′): in years 20–22 you convince
one person, at least as effective as you, who would otherwise have done nothing, and you do nothing after year 22; by stipulation
they do at least as much good as you would have done after year 22 in (1′). Exactly as before, (2′) ≥ (1′) "by definition", and
the argument's own inference then yields: *years 20–22 are more valuable than all of the rest of your life after them*. The same
construction works for years 40–42. A schema that ranks *every* two-year window above everything that comes after it cannot
establish that earlier windows are more valuable than later ones; it establishes only that the activity "recruit a replacement"
has a lifetime-sized payoff whenever the stipulation is satisfied. Nothing in the premises is indexed to "now".

**What the premises actually support.** Under the argument's own stipulations, the value of recruiting at time t equals the value
of one person's direct work from t + 2 to the end of a career, i.e. it declines linearly with remaining working life. The value of
two years of direct work at time t is two years of direct work, constant per year. So (a) recruiting dominates direct work at
every t, and (b) the ratio between the two does not favour the present in any way that would justify "vastly" or "more valuable
than all of the rest of your life": the only sense in which earlier is better is the trivial fact that more of a career remains,
which applies identically to direct work and therefore gives no guidance about *what to do when*. The haste consideration, as
stated ("resources are vastly more valuable sooner"), is simply not what the comparison delivers.

**The practical implication collapses with it.** "If a career requires you to spend the next few years studying and doing nothing
altruistic, this is a major cost" is true only if those years would otherwise be spent on the high-leverage activity. But by the
argument's own logic that is equally true of *any* years: the opportunity cost of study at 22 and at 42 is, under the stipulation,
the same lifetime-equivalent recruit (minus the trivial remaining-life effect). The argument gives no reason to weight the next few
years specially; at most it says "recruit at some point", and is silent on when.

**A tension inside the argument.** The entire value credited to the two present years in world (2) is produced by the recruit's
*future* decades of work. The premise therefore presupposes that future time is extremely valuable, while the conclusion says
present time is worth more than future time. The comparison cannot be read as evidence that time now beats time later, because
"time now" is being valued precisely by the amount of "time later" it purchases.

**The natural repair does not rescue the passage.** One might reply that the real haste consideration is about compounding: recruits
recruit, so early resources grow exponentially and earlier is genuinely better. But the passage explicitly switches compounding
off (you do nothing after two years; there is exactly one recruit, valued by their own direct output), and its author defends the
comparison as holding "by definition". If compounding is the argument, then (i) the thought experiment is idle, and (ii) the
argument now needs an empirical premise the passage never states or defends: a sustained growth rate well above the rate at
which opportunities to do good decline, with no saturation. That premise is exactly what is contested about movement-building
(returns saturate; movements grow logistically, not exponentially), so it cannot be smuggled in by a stipulation about a single
recruit. Either the stipulated comparison carries the conclusion, and then it carries the wrong conclusion (leverage, not haste);
or a growth premise carries it, and then the passage has not argued for it at all.

**Conclusion.** Granting every stipulation, the argument shows that converting two years into a lifetime-equivalent recruit beats
direct work whenever such a recruit is available. That is a claim about the *leverage of an activity*, not about the *value of a
time period*. The haste consideration is not established, the claim that the next two years outweigh the rest of a life is an
artefact of which activity was assigned to which world, and the career advice built on it has no support in the text.
